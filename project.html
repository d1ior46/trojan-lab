Machine-Learning Detection of Hardware Trojans in AES Designs

Catching malicious logic at the design stage — before the bitstream ever reaches an FPGA.

Amna Albalooshi · Meera Alsahlawi · Salama Alketbi  ·  Supervisor: Dr. Khalid Javeed

The threat, and what we built

FPGA designs reuse third-party IP and outsourced steps, so a design can be altered with a hardware Trojan before it reaches the developer. Trojans stay silent until a rare trigger, so they slip past normal testing. We build a machine-learning system that screens AES designs and flags infected ones at the design stage.

Anatomy of a hardware Trojan

Trigger a rare condition that activates it — here, one specific AES plaintext.

Payload the malicious action once triggered.

Our paired designs use a Trojan modelled on Trust-Hub AES-T1800 — a battery-drain Trojan. After the trigger, a 128-bit register rotates between 0xAAAA… and 0x5555… every clock, adding constant switching that burns power → denial of service.

▶ Launch the 3D hardware demo

See the payload in action on a 3D Basys 3 board: program a clean vs infected bitstream and watch the battery drain.

Why it's hard to catch

  • Functional testing fails: the trigger is rare, so a design passes its tests and stays infected.
  • The ciphertext is unchanged: this Trojan drains power without altering AES output — output checks see nothing.
  • The logic is tiny: its traces are spread across many small changes in cells, LUTs and switching — no single feature is a smoking gun.
Our idea: let a model weigh all 66 small indicators at once, from three complementary “views” of the same design.

Where a Trojan can enter the hardware lifecycle

Stage 1

Third-party IP cores integrated without full visibility.

Stage 2

Untrusted EDA tools that could modify a design silently.

Stage 3

The supply chain — many vendors and contractors.

Stage 4

Fabrication, after the design leaves the designer.

Three views, 66 numbers, one row per design

A Python extractor turns each design into 66 features from three files. Below: a clean AES core and its infected twin (same architecture — they differ only by the Trojan). Representative values.

Structural · 33

Cells, gate types, flip-flops, fan-in / fan-out from the Yosys netlist.

LUT · 23

How many LUTs of each size (2–6 inputs) and what their truth tables store.

Switching · 10

Signal toggles recorded in the simulation (VCD) — activity, not measured power.

Clean twin Infected twin
Both produce the identical ciphertext. The Trojan is invisible to an output check — the gap shows up in switching activity (and, slightly, in structure and LUTs).
